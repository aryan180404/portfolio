Boilers react late.

PID controllers and alarms act after the temperature has already moved. With long thermal lag, the correction arrives minutes too late.

Bigger swings
Steam temperature overshoots before the controller catches up.
Wasted spray water
Desuperheaters over-correct after the fact.
Tube stress
Repeated thermal cycling shortens superheater life.
Alarm fatigue
More limit crossings, more operator interventions.

A forecast, minutes ahead.

Kelvin Forward learns from the sensor history a plant already logs and forecasts critical variables 1, 5 and 10 minutes ahead. Operators and the existing loop act before the swing.

Software only
No new hardware. Reads the historian the plant already has.
Any control system
Not tied to one vendor’s platform.
Advisory first
PID loops, safety valves, interlocks and alarms stay exactly as they are.

How it works.

  1. Step 1

    Connect historian data

    An export is enough for a pilot. No new sensors.

  2. Step 2

    Clean and select inputs

    Repair gaps, drop faulty readings, prune inputs that repeat each other.

  3. Step 3

    Pick the best model per horizon

    Automatic selection across several model families, scored on unseen data.

  4. Step 4

    Deliver the forecast

    To operators as a trend running ahead, and to the loop as a feedforward signal.

Hybrid mode: keep the plant’s physics model; the forecast learns what it misses.

Data-driven mode: no model needed; it learns straight from sensor history.

Tested on a real boiler’s data.

Published dataset from a coal-fired boiler at a chemical plant in Zhejiang, China: 29 sensors, a reading every 5 seconds. Trained on four days, scored on the next day, which the models never saw.

Source: Hu et al., Scientific Data 12:742 (2025).

    Grey: assume the temperature stays where it is. Blue: the Kelvin Forward forecast. At 10 minutes the forecast explains less than half of the movement (R² 0.42); it still beats the baseline.
